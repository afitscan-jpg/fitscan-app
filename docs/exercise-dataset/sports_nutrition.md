Evidence-Based Sports Nutrition: A Comprehensive Knowledge Base of Dietary Practices for Athletic Performance and Body Composition
The Evolution and Foundation of Sports Nutrition
The optimization of athletic performance, muscular recovery, and body composition is fundamentally dependent upon the strategic application of nutritional science. Historically, athletic dietary practices were largely driven by anecdotal evidence, cultural traditions, and misconceptions regarding human metabolism. In previous eras, athletes employed counterproductive and often dangerous methods, such as the consumption of strychnine or severe dehydration, to alter performance. However, the modern discipline of sports nutrition has evolved into a rigorous, evidence-based scientific field governed by global consensus statements and position stands from leading authorities, including the International Society of Sports Nutrition (ISSN), the American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM), the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics (AND), the Dietitians of Canada (DC), and the International Olympic Committee (IOC).   
 
The prevailing consensus across these scientific organizations dictates that a uniform, one-size-fits-all approach to sports nutrition is entirely obsolete. Instead, optimal dietary strategies must be highly individualized, periodized, and specifically tailored to the bioenergetic demands of the sport, the phase of the training cycle, and the unique physiological characteristics of the athlete. The core pillars of this evidence-based framework encompass the careful management of total energy availability, the precise periodization of macronutrients, optimal hydration protocols, and the targeted application of safe, ergogenic dietary supplements.   
 
A critical component of this modern paradigm is the translation of scientific knowledge into athletic practice. Research indicates that many athletes possess significant gaps in their nutritional knowledge, leading to sub-optimal dietary practices that fail to meet recommendations for sports nutrition and general health. Utilizing validated assessment tools, such as the Sports Nutrition Knowledge Survey (SNKS), researchers have demonstrated that athletes with higher levels of nutritional knowledge are significantly more likely to adopt healthy dietary habits, which positively correlates with enhanced physical performance. Therefore, the foundational step in any athletic nutrition program is the education of the athlete and the coaching staff regarding the metabolic consequences of dietary choices.   
 
Energy Balance and Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport (REDs)
The Metabolic Framework of Energy Availability
The fundamental driver of both body composition alteration and sustained athletic performance is energy balance. However, in the context of high-performance sports science, this concept is more accurately defined and measured as Energy Availability (EA). EA represents the amount of metabolic energy remaining to support basic physiological functions, cellular maintenance, and overall health after subtracting the energy expended during physical activity.   
 
The mathematical formulation for Energy Availability is established in the bioenergetics literature as the difference between Energy Intake (EI) and Exercise Energy Expenditure (EEE), normalized to the athlete's Fat-Free Mass (FFM). For optimal physiological functioning, the maintenance of lean body mass, and robust athletic performance, an EA of approximately 45 kilocalories per kilogram of fat-free mass per day (kcal/kg FFM/day) is generally recommended across athletic populations. Conversely, when EA drops below a critical threshold—typically defined in the literature as less than 30 kcal/kg FFM/day—the human body initiates a profound evolutionary survival mechanism.   
 
The Pathophysiology and Systemic Impact of REDs
When the human body is subjected to prolonged or severe low energy availability (LEA), the hypothalamus—acting as the central energy sensor—begins to down-regulate physiological systems that it perceives as non-essential for immediate survival. This state of chronic LEA is the underlying etiology of a complex, multi-systemic syndrome known as Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport (REDs), a model originally proposed by an International Olympic Committee (IOC) expert panel in 2014 and comprehensively updated in 2023.   
 
The consequences of REDs extend far beyond the traditional "Female Athlete Triad," which focused primarily on the interrelationship between disordered eating, amenorrhea, and osteoporosis. The modern REDs model recognizes that severe energy deficiency causes deleterious health and performance outcomes in both male and female athletes. The physiological and psychological impacts of REDs are pervasive:   
 
Endocrine and Metabolic Suppression: High training volumes combined with LEA suppress the hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid axis. Studies consistently demonstrate that low energy availability leads to a dose-dependent reduction in circulating triiodothyronine (T3), a primary regulator of the metabolic rate. This hormonal disruption results in clinical symptoms such as severe lethargy, cold intolerance, weight loss resistance, and impaired glycogen storage, a cluster of adaptations clinically referred to as Low T3 Syndrome.   
 
Catabolic Hormonal Environment: Under conditions of extreme energy restriction, cortisol levels rise significantly while concentrations of insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1) plummet. This hormonal shift forces the body into a catabolic state that actively opposes skeletal muscle repair and hypertrophy. In the absence of adequate dietary fuel (particularly carbohydrates), the body upregulates gluconeogenesis, manufacturing glucose by stripping amino acids from skeletal muscle and collagen-rich connective tissues to maintain blood glucose supply to the central nervous system.   
 
Reproductive and Skeletal Health Impairments: LEA leads to severe reproductive dysfunction due to the suppression of the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis. In females, this manifests as primary or secondary amenorrhea (the absence of menstruation) or dysmenorrhea, while males experience a loss of morning erections and reduced libido. Concurrently, the disruption of sex hormones (estrogen and testosterone), combined with inadequate energy, leads to a failure to attain or maintain peak bone mass. This results in reduced bone mineral density, osteopenia, osteoporosis, and a significantly heightened risk of bone stress injuries and fractures.   
 
Immunological and Cardiovascular Deficits: Athletes suffering from REDs exhibit suppressed immune function, which drastically increases their susceptibility to upper respiratory tract infections (URTIs) and delays recovery times from minor illnesses. Furthermore, cardiovascular parameters are compromised, leading to impaired aerobic capacity, decreased endurance, and an overall reduction in the ability to tolerate high training loads.   
 
Psychological and Cognitive Consequences: The psychological indicators associated with problematic LEA and REDs are profound. Athletes frequently experience mood disturbances, increased anxiety (particularly related to injury and recovery), depressive symptoms, and cognitive dietary restraint. The drive for thinness and perfectionistic tendencies can precipitate or exacerbate disordered eating behaviors, ultimately leading to clinical eating disorders such as anorexia nervosa or bulimia nervosa.   
 
The prevalence of REDs is substantial across the athletic spectrum. Estimates suggest that 23% to 80% of female athletes and 15% to 70% of male athletes experience states of low energy availability, with the highest risks observed in endurance, aesthetic, and weight-class sports where a premium is placed on a low body mass or a specific physical appearance. To mitigate these severe risks, athletes must meticulously scale their energy and carbohydrate intakes directly to the fluctuating demands of their training phases, ensuring that energy expenditure is adequately matched by energy consumption.   
 
Dietary Archetypes and Body Composition Alteration
The strategic alteration of body composition—specifically the reduction of adipose tissue (fat mass) and the accretion or preservation of skeletal muscle (fat-free mass)—is a primary objective for numerous athletic populations. The International Society of Sports Nutrition (ISSN) provides an objective consensus regarding the efficacy of various dietary archetypes in achieving these morphological goals.

Before implementing a dietary strategy, it is crucial to accurately assess body composition. The ISSN notes that all body composition assessment methods possess inherent strengths and limitations, and they operate on a continuum of organizational levels.

The Primacy of the Caloric Deficit for Fat Loss
The fundamental physiological law governing fat loss across all dietary protocols is the requirement for a sustained caloric deficit. Whether an athlete adopts a low-fat diet, a low-carbohydrate/ketogenic diet, the Paleo diet, the Zone diet, or an intermittent fasting protocol, the singular mechanism driving the reduction in adipose tissue is negative energetic balance. Isocaloric comparisons of different dietary styles consistently demonstrate that when dietary protein and total calories are equated, there is no significant difference in total fat loss or lean mass retention between varying ratios of carbohydrates and fats.   
 
The aggressiveness of the caloric deficit should be inversely proportional to the athlete's baseline body fat percentage. Athletes with higher baseline body fat can safely sustain more aggressive caloric restrictions, whereas leaner athletes must utilize slower, more conservative rates of weight loss to preserve existing lean mass and prevent the onset of REDs.   
 
In clinical and obese populations, Very-Low-Energy Diets (VLED), providing 400 to 800 kcal/day, are sometimes employed to induce rapid weight loss. However, even with adequate protein intake, VLEDs can result in a loss of lean mass comprising up to 25% of the total weight lost. While resistance training can attenuate this muscle loss in untrained, obese subjects, VLEDs have limited relevance and are highly contraindicated for healthy and athletic populations due to the high risk of excessive lean mass loss, adaptive thermogenesis, and severe performance decrements.   
 
Ketogenic Diets and Athletic Performance
Ketogenic diets, characterized by severe carbohydrate restriction (typically less than 50 grams per day) and high dietary fat intake, induce a state of nutritional ketosis, generally defined as serum ketone levels exceeding 0.5 mM. While this dietary approach fundamentally alters substrate utilization—significantly elevating the rates of fat oxidation during exercise to approximately 1.5 grams per minute—this metabolic shift does not confer an advantage for high-intensity athletic performance.   
 
The ISSN position stand notes that the complete breakdown of glucose in the mitochondria yields adenosine triphosphate (ATP) at a faster rate and requires less oxygen per ATP produced than the oxidation of fatty acids. Consequently, ketogenic diets have consistently demonstrated neutral or detrimental effects on high-intensity and endurance athletic performance compared to carbohydrate-rich diets. The endurance effects of a ketogenic diet may be influenced by training status, but studies involving elite athletes have universally shown performance decrements during interventions lasting six weeks or less.   
 
Furthermore, while ketogenic diets often result in greater absolute weight loss on the scale, DXA analyses reveal that this is frequently accompanied by a disproportionate loss of lean body mass alongside fat mass. This heightened loss of lean tissue is likely due to shifts in total body water, profound glycogen depletion, and the absence of insulin-mediated anti-catabolic signaling. Therefore, for athletes engaged in sports requiring explosive power, sprinting, or high-intensity aerobic output, severe carbohydrate restriction is generally contraindicated.   
 
Intermittent Fasting and Time-Restricted Eating
Intermittent fasting (IF) and time-restricted eating (TRE) protocols limit caloric consumption to specific temporal windows (e.g., the 16:8 protocol involving 16 hours of fasting and an 8-hour feeding window). While highly popularized in secular fitness culture and often inaccurately marketed as a "metabolic hack," the collective body of scientific literature indicates that IF provides no significant metabolic or body composition advantages over continuous, evenly distributed daily caloric restriction.   
 
For the athletic population, IF introduces a severe physiological constraint: the restriction of the feeding window inherently limits the frequency of muscle protein synthesis stimulation. Given that muscle protein synthesis is maximally stimulated by discrete, evenly spaced protein feedings every 3 to 4 hours, an 8-hour feeding window sub-optimizes the physiological environment for muscle accretion and retention. Furthermore, research demonstrates a robust link between intermittent fasting and eating disorder behaviors in active populations, as the protocol acts as a strong predictor of binge eating and drastically increases the risk of inadvertent under-fueling and REDs.   
 
Macronutrient Requirements and Optimization
To facilitate metabolic adaptations, optimize sports performance, and drive post-exercise recovery, the precise calibration of macronutrients—protein, carbohydrates, and fats—is critical. The energy and nutrient needs of an athlete vary significantly across disciplines; an endurance runner has vastly different requirements than a powerlifter or a combat sports athlete.   
 
Dietary Protein: Requirements, Timing, and Quality
Dietary protein is the paramount macronutrient for the repair, maintenance, and hypertrophy of skeletal muscle and connective tissues. The ISSN and ACSM have thoroughly dispelled archaic myths suggesting that high-protein diets are detrimental to renal function or bone metabolism; strong evidence confirms that protein intakes well above the Recommended Dietary Allowance (RDA of 0.8 g/kg/day) are entirely safe and physiologically necessary for healthy, exercising individuals.   
 
Daily Protein Requirements
For most exercising individuals seeking to build or maintain muscle mass in a state of energy balance, an overall daily protein intake in the range of 1.4 to 2.0 grams per kilogram of body weight per day (g/kg/day) is highly effective and sufficient.   
 
However, this requirement scales upward significantly during periods of caloric restriction. To maximize the retention of lean body mass when operating in a hypocaloric state, resistance-trained athletes must escalate their protein intake to 2.3 to 3.1 g/kg/day. Furthermore, emerging evidence indicates that highly hypercaloric protein intakes exceeding 3.0 g/kg/day may exert unique positive effects on body composition. Studies on advanced resistance-trained individuals have demonstrated that such extreme protein intakes do not lead to fat accumulation, but rather promote the loss of fat mass while simultaneously upregulating resting energy expenditure via the thermic effect of food.   
 
Protein Pacing and Timing
The metabolic machinery governing muscle growth does not operate on a simple 24-hour continuous cycle; rather, it is exquisitely sensitive to the acute availability of amino acids in the bloodstream. Consequently, the temporal distribution of protein—termed "protein pacing"—is a critical variable for optimizing adaptation.   
 
An acute exercise stimulus, particularly resistance training, dramatically increases the sensitivity of the muscle to protein ingestion for at least 24 hours. To maximally stimulate muscle protein synthesis (MPS), athletes should ingest 0.25 to 0.40 grams of high-quality protein per kilogram of body weight per meal, which typically equates to an absolute dose of 20 to 40 grams of protein. Because the MPS response to a protein feeding is transient (returning to baseline after roughly 3 hours despite sustained elevations in plasma amino acids), these 20–40 gram doses should be evenly distributed every 3 to 4 hours throughout the day to maximize the total daily anabolic response.   
 
The Role of Leucine and Protein Quality
The efficacy of a protein dose is fundamentally dictated by its essential amino acid (EAA) profile, and specifically, its leucine content. Leucine acts as a direct anabolic trigger for the mammalian target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1) pathway, the primary molecular cascade responsible for initiating protein translation and muscle growth. Acute protein feedings should strive to contain 700 to 3,000 mg of leucine to optimally flip this molecular switch.   
 
Rapidly digested proteins containing high proportions of EAAs and adequate leucine, such as whey protein isolate, are highly effective at acutely spiking plasma amino acid levels and stimulating MPS immediately following exercise. Conversely, micellar casein is digested at a much slower rate, resulting in a prolonged, moderate elevation of plasma amino acids that can be sustained for up to seven hours. The strategic ingestion of 30 to 40 grams of casein protein 30 to 60 minutes prior to sleep capitalizes on this slow digestion rate, significantly enhancing overnight MPS and resting metabolic rate without impeding lipolysis (fat burning) during the fasting period of sleep.   
 
Athletes following plant-based diets or utilizing alternative protein sources must be cognizant of protein quality. While plant proteins often possess inferior EAA profiles and lower digestibility scores compared to animal proteins, combining multiple complementary plant sources or utilizing emerging alternatives like protein-dense press cakes can help achieve the necessary leucine and EAA thresholds for optimal recovery.   
 
Carbohydrates: The Primary Athletic Fuel
Carbohydrates are the body's preferred and most metabolically efficient source of energy during moderate to high-intensity exercise. During the digestive process, complex carbohydrates are enzymatically broken down into glucose, which is released into the bloodstream. Excess glucose is converted into glycogen by the enzyme glycogen synthase and stored within skeletal muscle tissue and the liver.   
 
In contemporary sports science, muscle glycogen is no longer viewed merely as a passive energy reservoir. It is now understood to act as a potent signaling molecule that actively regulates molecular adaptations to exercise and communicates systemic energy status directly to the hypothalamus and neuroendocrine system. Insufficient carbohydrate availability independently drives hormonal disruption, impairs iron regulation, and suppresses immune function, separate from the effects of total caloric restriction.   
 
Daily Carbohydrate Scaling
Carbohydrate recommendations are highly variable and must be scaled directly to the athlete's training volume and intensity. The general baseline recommendation ranges from 5 to 12 g/kg/day. For moderate exercise (approximately 1 hour per day), intakes of 5 to 7 g/kg/day are sufficient. Elite athletes engaging in moderate to high-intensity exercise for 1 to 3 hours per day require 6 to 10 g/kg/day, while ultra-endurance athletes participating in multi-hour events may require up to 8 to 12 g/kg/day to maintain adequate glycogen stores and prevent the rapid onset of fatigue.   
 
Intra-Workout Fueling and Nutrient Timing
For exercise sessions extending beyond 60 minutes at intensities greater than 70% VO2 max, endogenous glycogen stores become severely challenged, leading to a precipitous drop in performance. Under these conditions, the ACSM and ISSN recommend the continuous ingestion of 30 to 60 grams of carbohydrate per hour, typically delivered via a 6–8% carbohydrate-electrolyte solution consumed every 10 to 15 minutes.   
 
For elite endurance athletes (e.g., marathon runners, triathletes, and touring paddleboarders), optimal performance often requires aggressively pushing this upper limit to 60–90 grams of carbohydrate per hour. Because the maximal gastrointestinal absorption rate of a single carbohydrate source (e.g., glucose) is rate-limited to approximately 1.0 g/min (60 g/hour) by the SGLT1 intestinal transporter, athletes requiring >60 g/hour must consume multiple transportable carbohydrates (e.g., a glucose and fructose blend). This strategy engages simultaneous intestinal transporters (SGLT1 and GLUT5), maximizing carbohydrate oxidation rates while preventing severe gastrointestinal distress.   
 
Post-Exercise Glycogen Replenishment
In scenarios where athletes have multiple training sessions or competitions separated by less than 4 to 8 hours, rapid glycogen restoration is a physiological imperative. Glycogen synthase enzyme activity peaks immediately after exercise, making the 30–60 minute post-exercise window the most efficient time to refuel. The optimal strategy involves an aggressive carbohydrate refeeding rate of 1.0 to 1.2 g/kg/hour, prioritizing high-glycemic-index (>70) carbohydrate sources to induce a rapid insulin response.   
 
The co-ingestion of protein (0.2–0.4 g/kg/hour) alongside carbohydrates (0.8 g/kg/hour) has been shown to facilitate rapid glycogen resynthesis equivalently to high-carbohydrate alone, while concurrently initiating muscle tissue repair and improving net protein balance. Furthermore, the addition of caffeine (3–8 mg/kg) to a post-exercise carbohydrate load has demonstrated utility in accelerating glycogen restoration kinetics.   
 
Dietary Fats: Essential Regulators
While fats are oxidized as a primary fuel source only during low-intensity, aerobic exercise, they serve critical structural and hormonal functions that are indispensable for athletic health. Dietary fats are imperative for the synthesis of steroid hormones (including testosterone and estrogen), the absorption of fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K), the formation of cell membranes, and the regulation of the nervous system.   
 
Athletes should maintain a daily fat intake constituting 20% to 35% of total caloric intake. Diets restricting fat intake below 15% to 20% of total calories provide no performance benefits and actively compromise health, hormonal stability, and immune function. Saturated fats should be limited to less than 10% of total energy intake, with the balance sourced from nutrient-dense monounsaturated and polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs).   
 
Attempts to utilize "fat loading" strategies to enhance endurance performance have largely proven ineffective. While adapting to a high-fat diet (e.g., >65% of calories from fat for 5 days) does increase the capacity for intramuscular triglyceride oxidation, this strategy severely downregulates the rates of muscle glycogen utilization. Given that high-intensity power generation and late-stage endurance kicks rely heavily on rapid carbohydrate oxidation, the suppression of glycogen utilization renders fat-loading counterproductive for most competitive sports.   
 
Omega-3 Fatty Acids
Omega-3 PUFAs—specifically eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA)—warrant special attention in athletic populations. Omega-3s act as powerful structural components of cell membranes and profound modulators of the body's inflammatory response. Supplementation at clinical dosages (e.g., 2,400 mg/day of combined EPA+DHA) has been shown to significantly reduce inflammatory markers (such as IL-6, TNF-α, and CRP) following exercise-induced muscle damage, mitigate delayed-onset muscle soreness (DOMS), enhance anaerobic endurance capacity, and improve oxygen efficiency.   
 
Hydration Dynamics and Fluid Replacement
Dehydration represents one of the most immediate and profound threats to athletic performance and health. Water constitutes approximately 60% of human body mass and serves as the primary medium for nutrient transport, joint lubrication, and thermoregulation via the evaporation of sweat.   
 
The Physiology of Dehydration and Cardiovascular Drift
During vigorous exercise, particularly in warm or humid environments, the evaporation of sweat is the major avenue for cooling the core body temperature. Sweat rates can vary enormously between individuals, ranging from 0.3 to 2.4 liters per hour, heavily dependent upon genetics, acclimatization status, exercise intensity, and ambient conditions. As the body loses fluid, plasma volume decreases, resulting in a state of hypohydration.   
 
This reduction in plasma volume decreases venous return to the heart, causing a drop in stroke volume. To maintain cardiac output and sustain oxygen delivery to the working muscles, heart rate must disproportionately increase—a phenomenon known as cardiovascular drift. Simultaneously, the loss of fluid impairs the body's ability to shunt blood to the skin for evaporative cooling, resulting in a rapid elevation of core body temperature and an increased perception of effort.   
 
The ACSM and numerous physiological studies dictate that fluid losses exceeding 2% to 3% of an athlete's total body mass yield a precipitous decline in aerobic capacity (by up to 10–20%), cognitive function, muscular strength, and multi-set work capacity.   
 
Structured Hydration Protocols
To prevent the deleterious effects of hypovolemia, athletes must adhere to structured, individualized fluid replacement protocols rather than relying solely on the sensation of thirst, which is often blunted during intense exercise and lags significantly behind actual physiological fluid deficits. Assessment of hydration status in the field is best achieved by monitoring acute changes in body mass, assessing urine color, or utilizing more precise biological markers such as urine specific gravity (USG) or salivary osmolarity. Euhydration is typically defined by a USG of ≤ 1.020.   
 
Pre-Exercise Hydration
The objective of pre-hydration is to begin the activity fully euhydrated and with normalized plasma electrolyte concentrations. The ACSM recommends consuming 5 to 7 mL of fluid per kilogram of body weight (mL/kg) approximately 4 hours prior to exercise. If urine output remains low, or if the USG indicates concentrated urine, an additional bolus of 3 to 5 mL/kg should be ingested 2 hours before the session, ideally alongside a sodium source to stimulate thirst and retain fluid.   
 
Intra-Exercise Fluid and Electrolyte Replacement
During exercise, athletes should aim to consume fluids at a rate commensurate with their individual sweat losses to limit total body mass loss to less than 2%.   
 
Crucially, sweat is hypotonic to plasma, meaning it contains a significant concentration of electrolytes, primarily sodium. The ingestion of massive quantities of plain, hypotonic water during prolonged exercise (>1 hour) without concomitant sodium replacement dilutes serum sodium concentrations. This osmotic shift drives fluid into the intracellular space, causing cellular swelling and precipitating a potentially fatal condition known as Exercise-Associated Hyponatremia (EAH) or hypervolaemic hypernatraemic encephalopathy.   
 
To safeguard against EAH, support the retention of ingested fluids, and maintain the thirst drive, intra-workout beverages should contain sodium at concentrations of 0.5 to 0.7 grams per liter (roughly 20 to 30 mmol/L). In extreme endurance events, sodium intake requirements may escalate to 300 to 600 mg per hour.   
 
Post-Exercise Rehydration
Following training or competition, the objective is to fully correct the accumulated fluid and electrolyte deficit. Because the kidneys continue to excrete urine during the recovery phase, athletes must consume a fluid volume greater than what was lost. The standard protocol dictates ingesting 125% to 150% of the total acute fluid deficit (e.g., consuming 1.25 to 1.5 liters of fluid for every 1 kilogram of body weight lost). This fluid must be co-ingested with sodium and other electrolytes to maximize fluid retention, restore plasma volume, and prevent excess diuresis.   
 
Combat Sports and Weight Cutting Strategies
Athletes in weight-class sports (e.g., wrestling, judo, mixed martial arts) frequently manipulate their diets and hydration status to qualify for specific weight categories, a practice known as "making weight." The physiological toll of rapid weight loss can be severe, necessitating highly strategic, evidence-based approaches to minimize performance decrements.   
 
During the general preparation phase, athletes should maintain a body mass no more than 12% to 15% above their competition weight class. During the "fight camp" phase, sustained caloric deficits are employed to gradually reduce fat mass, but protein must be prioritized (1.2–2.0 g/kg/day) to preserve lean tissue, and carbohydrates (3.0–4.0 g/kg/day) must be maintained to support high-intensity training.   
 
Acute Weight Cut Strategies
In the final week preceding a weigh-in, athletes manipulate body water and gastrointestinal contents to induce acute, rapid weight loss. Strategic depletion of water-bound glycogen stores and the implementation of a low-fiber diet (< 10 g/day for 4 days) can facilitate a 1% to 2% reduction in total body mass without significant metabolic cost.   
 
In the final 24 to 48 hours, active and passive sweating protocols (e.g., saunas, hot water immersion) combined with severe sodium restriction and water loading (which induces polyuria) are utilized to shed an additional 2% to 4% of body mass. Safe thresholds for total body mass loss range from 6.7% at 72 hours prior to weigh-in to 4.4% at 24 hours prior.   
 
Post-Weigh-in Rehydration and Refueling
The post-weigh-in period is a critical window to reverse the physiological damage of the weight cut. Immediately following the weigh-in, athletes must prioritize the consumption of oral rehydration solutions (ORS) at a rate of 1.0 to 1.5 liters per hour, containing sodium concentrations of 50 to 90 mmol/L to rapidly restore plasma volume.   
 
Once rehydration is initiated, athletes must aggressively replenish depleted glycogen stores by consuming fast-acting, high-glycemic carbohydrates at a tolerable rate of ≤ 60 g/hour. Total post-weigh-in carbohydrate intake should target 8 to 12 g/kg of body weight to restore explosive muscular power. Fiber and high-fat foods should be strictly avoided during this window to prevent severe gastrointestinal distress.   
 
Evidence-Based Ergogenic Aids and Supplements
While a robust, periodized, food-first nutritional foundation is non-negotiable, the strategic application of dietary supplements can confer significant competitive advantages. The IOC and ISSN categorize supplements based on a rigorous risk-versus-benefit analysis and the strength of the scientific evidence supporting their efficacy and safety. The following substances possess "Strong Evidence to Support Efficacy and Apparently Safe" classifications and have been demonstrated to directly enhance athletic performance.   
 
Creatine Monohydrate
Creatine monohydrate is unequivocally the most extensively researched and consistently effective ergogenic nutritional supplement available for increasing high-intensity exercise capacity and lean body mass.   
 
Mechanism of Action: Creatine is a naturally occurring guanidine phosphagen synthesized in the liver and kidneys and found primarily in skeletal muscle. Upon entering the muscle cell, it is phosphorylated by the enzyme creatine kinase to form phosphocreatine (PCr). During explosive, maximal-effort exercise (e.g., sprinting, heavy weightlifting, jumping), the rapid degradation of ATP limits performance. The massive intracellular pool of PCr acts as a rapid phosphate donor, instantaneously resynthesizing ATP and significantly delaying the onset of muscular failure.   
 
Efficacy and Benefits: Creatine supplementation increases intramuscular creatine concentrations by up to 30%, translating to direct improvements in maximal strength, power output, sprint performance, and training volume capacity, which ultimately drives greater muscle hypertrophy. Beyond the musculoskeletal system, an emerging body of evidence confirms that creatine exhibits profound neuroprotective properties, improves cognitive processing under sleep-deprived conditions, and accelerates recovery following traumatic brain injuries or concussions.   
 
Dosing and Safety: Muscle creatine saturation can be achieved via two scientifically validated protocols:
 
Loading Phase: Ingestion of ~20 grams per day (or 0.3 g/kg/day), divided into four 5-gram doses, for 5 to 7 days, followed by a maintenance dose of 3 to 5 grams daily. This achieves full intramuscular saturation rapidly.   
 
Non-Loading Phase: Consistent ingestion of 3 to 5 grams per day. This protocol takes roughly 28 days to reach full muscle saturation but entirely avoids the potential gastrointestinal discomfort associated with loading.   
 
Decades of rigorous clinical trials have thoroughly debunked myths regarding creatine's supposed negative impact on renal function, liver health, or its alleged propensity to cause cramping and dehydration. The ISSN officially concludes that both short-term and long-term supplementation (up to 30 grams per day for 5 years) is extremely safe and well-tolerated in healthy populations across the lifespan, ranging from infants to the elderly.   
 
Caffeine
Caffeine is a potent central nervous system stimulant utilized globally to reduce the perception of effort, delay fatigue, and enhance physical output.   
 
Mechanism of Action: Caffeine's primary ergogenic mechanism is the competitive antagonism of adenosine A1 and A2A receptors in the central nervous system. By blocking adenosine from binding to its receptors, caffeine prevents the neurochemical signaling of fatigue and sleepiness, thereby increasing alertness, vigilance, and motor unit recruitment. At the peripheral muscular level, caffeine also alters calcium ion sensitivity within the sarcoplasmic reticulum, increasing the mobilization of calcium and enhancing the force of muscle contractions.   
 
Efficacy and Dosing: Caffeine administration provides small to moderate performance enhancements across a vast spectrum of activities, including muscular endurance, maximal strength, sprinting, and prolonged aerobic endurance. The optimal, evidence-based dose is consistently established at 3 to 6 milligrams per kilogram of body mass (mg/kg), ingested approximately 45 to 60 minutes prior to exercise.   
 
While lower doses (e.g., 2 mg/kg) may provide cognitive benefits, exceedingly high doses (greater than 9 mg/kg) do not offer additional ergogenic effects and significantly increase the risk of severe adverse side effects, including tachycardia, gastrointestinal distress, extreme anxiety, and crippling insomnia. Because the half-life of caffeine is substantial, practitioners must carefully periodize its use during evening competitions to prevent the disruption of sleep architecture, which is critical for systemic recovery.   
 
The metabolism of caffeine varies widely between individuals, largely dictated by genetic variations in the cytochrome P450 1A2 (CYP1A2) enzyme. Consequently, caffeine can be delivered via various modalities—including anhydrous capsules, caffeinated chewing gum (which absorbs rapidly through the buccal mucosa), mouth rinses, and energy gels—to suit individual tolerance and timing requirements. Energy drinks and pre-workout supplements containing caffeine are effective, but athletes must be cautious of the cumulative daily intake of stimulants and the presence of high-glycemic carbohydrates in these formulations.   
 
Intracellular and Extracellular Buffers
High-intensity anaerobic exercise (e.g., a 400-meter sprint, a judo match, or a high-rep resistance training set) relies heavily on the glycolytic energy system. This system rapidly produces ATP but results in the concurrent accumulation of metabolic byproducts, specifically hydrogen ions (H 
+
 ). The subsequent drop in intracellular pH (acidosis) inhibits glycolytic enzymes, disrupts calcium binding, and acts as the primary molecular trigger for peripheral muscular fatigue. Dietary buffers neutralize this acidosis, extending the duration of high-intensity output.   
 
Beta-Alanine (Intracellular Buffer)
Beta-alanine is a non-essential amino acid that serves as the rate-limiting precursor to the synthesis of the dipeptide carnosine (β-alanyl-L-histidine) within skeletal muscle. Carnosine acts as a highly effective intracellular physiochemical buffer (more effective than bicarbonate or inorganic phosphate within the physiological pH range), sequestering hydrogen ions generated during high-intensity exercise.   
 
Dosing and Efficacy: The ISSN position stand confirms that chronic supplementation of 4 to 6 grams of beta-alanine per day for a minimum of 4 weeks increases intramuscular carnosine concentrations by 40% to 64% (and up to 80% after 10 weeks). This amplified buffering capacity yields significant ergogenic benefits specifically in high-intensity exercises spanning 60 to 240 seconds, making it highly effective for combat athletes, rowers, and middle-distance runners.   
 
A known, benign side effect of acute high-dose beta-alanine ingestion is paresthesia (a transient tingling sensation on the skin). This is mediated by the activation of MrgD G-protein coupled receptors located in the dorsal root ganglion that terminate in the skin. This effect is entirely harmless and can be mitigated by dividing the total daily intake into smaller doses (e.g., 1 to 2 grams per serving) or utilizing sustained-release formulations. While animal models suggested high-dose beta-alanine might deplete taurine (as they share the Tau-T transporter), human trials have shown no physiologically significant decrease in taurine with standard supplementation protocols.

This protocol significantly enhances performance in muscular endurance tasks, combat sports (judo, boxing, wrestling), and high-intensity cycling, running, and swimming. Higher acute doses (0.4 or 0.5 g/kg) do not provide additional benefits and drastically increase the incidence and severity of gastrointestinal distress (osmotic diarrhea, bloating, and cramping). Alternatively, athletes can utilize a multi-day loading protocol (0.4 to 0.5 g/kg per day, divided into small doses with meals for 3 to 7 days prior to competition) to achieve the ergogenic effect while minimizing acute GI issues. Combining sodium bicarbonate with beta-alanine or creatine has been shown to produce additive, synergistic effects on performance.   
 
Beta-Hydroxy-Beta-Methylbutyrate (HMB)
Beta-hydroxy-beta-methylbutyrate (HMB) is a metabolite of the essential amino acid leucine that exerts profound anti-catabolic effects on skeletal muscle.   
 
Mechanism and Efficacy: HMB functions by simultaneously inhibiting proteolysis (muscle breakdown) and stimulating protein synthesis. The ISSN confirms that HMB supplementation effectively attenuates exercise-induced skeletal muscle damage, enhances recovery, and promotes increases in lean body mass, strength, and power, particularly when athletes are subjected to high training volumes or operating in a caloric deficit.   
 
Dosing: The standard, scientifically validated dosage is 38 milligrams per kilogram of body mass daily (typically around 3 grams per day for an average adult), ingested in close proximity to the training bout. HMB is available in two forms: Calcium HMB (HMB-Ca) and a free acid form (HMB-FA). While early research suggests HMB-FA may increase plasma absorption and retention to a greater extent, both forms are highly effective. To maximize efficacy, supplementation should ideally begin two weeks prior to a period of intense training.

Micronutrients Critical for Athletic Function
While macronutrients provide the raw energetic substrates and structural building blocks, micronutrients function as the indispensable enzymatic co-factors that facilitate energy metabolism, oxygen transport, hormone synthesis, and tissue repair. Athletes who restrict energy intake to "make weight," eliminate entire food groups, or engage in rigorous endurance training are highly susceptible to severe micronutrient deficiencies, which can catastrophically impair performance and long-term health.   
 
Vitamin D
Vitamin D is a fat-soluble secosteroid hormone with a systemic influence that extends far beyond the traditional confines of calcium homeostasis and bone metabolism. Vitamin D receptors (VDR) are ubiquitously expressed across physiological systems, including skeletal muscle tissue, cardiac myocytes, fibroblasts, and immune cells.   
 
Deficiency in Vitamin D (defined as serum 25(OH)D levels < 20 ng/mL) directly impairs cardiac contractility, upregulates the parathyroid hormone (PTH)—which can lead to left ventricular hypertrophy—and increases arterial stiffness and endothelial dysfunction. In athletic populations, this deficiency manifests as a severe reduction in aerobic capacity, diminished muscular power, and a drastically heightened vulnerability to soft tissue injuries, bone stress fractures, and upper respiratory tract infections (URTIs).   
 
Athletes should routinely monitor their serum 25(OH)D status, aiming for a sufficiency target of > 30 ng/mL (and ideally > 40 ng/mL for optimal function), while avoiding toxicity thresholds (> 100 ng/mL). If dietary intake (e.g., fortified dairy, fish, mushrooms) and ultraviolet B (UVB) sunlight exposure are insufficient, daily supplementation of 1,000 to 5,000 IU of Vitamin D3 (cholecalciferol) is strongly recommended. Vitamin D3 is significantly more efficacious at raising serum concentrations than Vitamin D2 (ergocalciferol).   
 
Iron and Oxygen Transport
Iron is an integral structural component of hemoglobin and myoglobin, strictly dictating the blood and muscle's oxygen-carrying capacity. Heavy training loads—particularly endurance running and high-altitude training—drastically increase iron turnover due to hemolysis (the mechanical destruction of red blood cells from foot-strike impact), heavy sweating, and microscopic gastrointestinal bleeding.   
 
Iron deficiency, even in the absence of clinical anemia, severely limits aerobic performance, disrupts energy metabolism, and exacerbates fatigue. Athletes training at altitude to stimulate erythropoiesis (the production of red blood cells) must ensure impeccable iron status, as the increased synthesis of red blood cells drastically increases the physiological demand for iron, vitamin B12, and folic acid. The recommended supplementation protocol to support altitude adaptations is 100 to 200 mg of elemental iron daily, closely monitored by a physician.   
 
Calcium and Bone Integrity
Calcium is paramount for structural bone integrity and is the primary mineral responsible for initiating optimal neuromuscular signaling and muscle contraction. In athletes suffering from REDs, the endocrine suppression of estrogen and testosterone triggers rapid bone resorption. Without sufficient dietary calcium intake to counteract this physiological cascade, the athlete is placed at immediate risk for debilitating stress fractures and irreversible long-term osteopenia. Athletes, particularly adolescents in periods of rapid growth and peak height velocity, must ensure robust calcium intake through fortified foods, dairy, or targeted supplementation to safeguard skeletal health under heavy impact loads.
